How to do it

Setup

Stand with your feet roughly shoulder-width apart, toes pointed very slightly outward. Weight spread across the whole foot rather than the toes. Arms can reach forward as a counterbalance.

The movement

Push your hips back and bend the knees together, lowering until your thighs are at least parallel to the floor, deeper if your ankles and hips allow it. Keep your chest up and your heels down. Drive back up through the middle of the foot, squeezing the glutes at the top without leaning back.

Common mistakes

  • Knees collapsing inward on the way up, which is usually weak glutes rather than bad knees
  • Heels lifting, which is generally an ankle mobility limitation
  • Quarter reps: stopping well above parallel and adding numbers instead of depth
  • Rounding the lower back at the bottom, often from forcing depth the hips do not yet have

Too hard? Scale it down

Squat to a chair or a box, tapping it lightly rather than sitting. It gives you a depth target and somewhere to bail. Holding a doorframe for light assistance also works while you build confidence in the bottom position.

Too easy? Scale it up

Slow the descent to three seconds, pause at the bottom, or progress toward the Bulgarian split squat and the pistol squat, both of which are further up the same pool.

Squats FAQ

How deep should I squat?

At least thighs parallel to the floor. Deeper is fine and often better if you can hold a neutral spine and keep your heels down. Depth you have to round your back to reach is not depth worth having.

Are squats bad for your knees?

For healthy knees, no. Well-executed squats are generally used to build knee resilience rather than damage it. Pain during squatting is worth having assessed rather than trained through.

Why do my heels come up?

Usually restricted ankle dorsiflexion. Elevating the heels slightly on a small plate or book is a legitimate short-term fix while you work on ankle mobility.
